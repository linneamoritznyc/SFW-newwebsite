## PART 5 — HOW THEY WORK: BUILD, TECH, AND COLLABORATION

Read out of the above, for whoever picks this up in a new chat.

### The collaboration pattern

There is no design owner. Evan holds vision, structure, copy and content but says plainly he has no aesthetic vision — only the ability to recognise when something looks bad or looks AI-generated. Alex was assumed to be the creative lead because he said he likes design; he isn't, and his role is now scoped to technical implementation. Stephanie has been filling the aesthetic gap on top of an already full plate. The current palette exists because she was handed an incongruent set of guidelines and tried to unify them — Evan calls this an inertia problem and says explicitly that this is the moment to change it.

The approval path Evan named: **Linnea and Evan weigh in, sign off and approve → Alex implements.** He called this "the most likely path to success."

### How tech decisions get made

Conservatively, and around a small number of fragile live systems. The GitHub account holds production products including the microscopy app, and because Claude Code requires whole-account access, connecting it was ruled out inside a minute. Company Claude is a shared alias login across ~18 people rather than per-seat purchases. Company Claude is not connected to company Drive. The pattern: shared cheap access, hard walls around anything live.

This means the build must be self-contained and handover-ready. It cannot assume repo access, CMS access, or anything touching production.

### Known failure modes to design against

Everything broken on the current site traces to one of two causes: nobody owns maintenance, or updating content is too hard. The sold-out workshop link stayed live for weeks. The pathway page was supposed to be deleted months ago. Directory records required manual reshuffling on delete. Retired programs are still listed. Evan was surprised by his own site during the call because he doesn't look at it.

A beautiful static build that is hard to edit reproduces the exact problem. Editability is not a nice-to-have here; it's the thing that failed.

### What "sway him" actually means

Evan's stated bar is low and specific: it should look nice, and it should not look AI-generated. That is the whole aesthetic brief. The persuasion is not in visual polish for its own sake — it's in showing him a site that solves the things he complained about while he was clicking.

Concretely, a demo lands hardest if it:

- **Fixes the nine-item header in front of him.** Show the reduced nav. It's the most concrete complaint he made and the easiest thing to feel.
- **Demonstrates the hero-led → community-led shift visually.** Faces of the movement instead of Elaine's face. He described this as the core brand shift, not a design preference.
- **Surfaces the buried content.** Case studies, media, videos — he said it was sad how hard they were to find. Putting them one click from the homepage is a visible win.
- **Shows the "are they still active" hub.** This was your idea and he didn't have a name for it. Building it and naming it makes it yours.
- **Rewrites the claims into referenced form.** Take "water requirements slashed by 50%" and show it attached to a source the way the South Africa tomato example is. This addresses a board-level concern, not a design one.
- **Shows the workshops page that doesn't exist.** Past workshops, what they are, what's coming.
- **Uses only owned assets.** Stephanie's photo/video spreadsheet, video titles as placeholders. He confirmed placeholders are fine.
- **Picks one palette and commits.** Don't present options. He said he doesn't want to adjudicate shades of green — he wants someone to decide. Presenting three palettes hands the decision back to him and stalls again.

### How to present it

Short. He asked directly not to be sent dozens of pages of AI output. Show the working site, not a document about the site. Let him click.

The one thing to say out loud: this is a proposal, not a build — if you like it, we build it properly. That was your own framing in the call and he agreed to it immediately.

### Timing

Evan leaves for India in early October and named the website as the first big thing in front of him, ahead of contracts and the RySS work. The demo lands best before that trip, while there's still room to iterate.
